The line forms before dawn. By the time the sun clears the customs roof, it
stretches past the bakery, past the shuttered cinema, past the mural that someone
repaints every spring.

## The economy of the queue

Everything in this town has rearranged itself around the wait. The bakery opens at
four. A man sells phone chargers and folding stools from a cart. A woman has
memorized the moods of every guard on the morning shift and sells that knowledge,
gently, as advice.

> "You learn the border like you learn weather," she said. "Not to control it. To
> survive it."

### Three lives, one line

- **Tomás** crosses every day to work and back. The wait is his commute; he has
  read more books in this queue than he did in school.
- **Amara** is waiting for papers that may take a year. She runs the stool
  business in the meantime.
- **The guard**, who would not give his name, has worked the crossing for eleven
  years. "People think we decide things," he said. "We mostly just open and close
  a gate someone else built."

## What the maps leave out

On a map the border is a clean line. On the ground it is a thick, living band — a
zone with its own economy, its own customs, its own grammar of patience. The
people here do not experience the border as a line at all. They experience it as
*time*: hours lost, hours sold, hours waited.

The town does not resent the wait, exactly. It has simply absorbed it, the way a
body absorbs an old injury and learns to walk around it.
