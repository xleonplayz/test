When the bulldozers finished, the engineers expected the river to take a
generation to recover. It took three years.

## A controlled experiment, run by accident

Removing the dam was a decision about safety, not science. The structure was old
and cracking, and rebuilding it would have cost more than tearing it down. But the
removal handed ecologists something they almost never get: a before-and-after of
an entire watershed.

The sediment moved first. Then the fish.

> "We modeled decades," one researcher told me, standing on the new gravel bank.
> "The river didn't read the model."

### What the data showed

- Salmon returned to spawning grounds upstream within **two seasons**.
- Sediment that had pooled behind the dam redistributed downstream, rebuilding
  habitat that had been starved for half a century.
- Bird populations tracking the fish followed within a year.

The recovery was not uniform. Some side channels stayed silted and lifeless.
Recovery, it turns out, is *patchy* — a mosaic, not a wave.

## The uncomfortable lesson

The temptation is to read this as a feel-good story, and in part it is. But the
scientists are careful. A river that recovers quickly when freed says nothing
about a river poisoned slowly over decades. The lesson is narrower and sharper:

When you remove the thing causing the harm, sometimes nature does the rest faster
than anyone dares to predict. The hard part is removing the thing.
